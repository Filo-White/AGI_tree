Abstract

This paper presents a comprehensive review of reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms and their applications in robotics. We analyze the evolution of RL methods from tabular approaches to modern deep reinforcement learning, discussing their theoretical foundations, practical implementations, and current limitations. Our survey covers model-free and model-based methods, policy gradient techniques, and actor-critic architectures. We provide empirical comparisons on standard benchmarks and discuss open challenges in sample efficiency, sim-to-real transfer, and safe exploration.

1. Introduction

Reinforcement learning has emerged as one of the most promising paradigms for enabling autonomous agents to learn complex behaviors through interaction with their environment. Unlike supervised learning, which requires labeled datasets, RL agents learn from trial and error, receiving reward signals that guide their behavior toward optimal policies.

The application of RL to robotics presents unique challenges. Physical robots operate in continuous state and action spaces, face partial observability, and must learn efficiently due to the high cost of real-world interactions. Despite these challenges, recent advances in deep RL have demonstrated remarkable capabilities, from dexterous manipulation to locomotion across complex terrains.

This survey is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews the theoretical foundations of RL. Section 3 discusses model-free methods including Q-learning and policy gradient approaches. Section 4 covers model-based RL. Section 5 examines actor-critic architectures. Section 6 presents applications in robotics. Section 7 discusses open challenges and future directions.

2. Theoretical Foundations

2.1 Markov Decision Processes

The standard framework for RL is the Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined as a tuple (S, A, P, R, γ), where S is the state space, A is the action space, P(s'|s,a) is the transition probability function, R(s,a) is the reward function, and γ ∈ [0,1] is the discount factor.

The goal of an RL agent is to find a policy π(a|s) that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted reward:

J(π) = E[Σ γ^t R(s_t, a_t)]

The value function V^π(s) represents the expected return starting from state s and following policy π. The action-value function Q^π(s,a) represents the expected return starting from state s, taking action a, and then following policy π.

2.2 Bellman Equations

The optimal value functions satisfy the Bellman optimality equations:

V*(s) = max_a [R(s,a) + γ Σ P(s'|s,a) V*(s')]
Q*(s,a) = R(s,a) + γ Σ P(s'|s,a) max_a' Q*(s',a')

These equations form the basis for dynamic programming approaches and many RL algorithms.

2.3 Exploration vs Exploitation

A fundamental challenge in RL is the exploration-exploitation dilemma. The agent must balance between exploiting known rewarding actions and exploring new actions that might lead to higher rewards. Common strategies include ε-greedy, Upper Confidence Bound (UCB), and Thompson Sampling.

3. Model-Free Methods

3.1 Q-Learning and Deep Q-Networks

Q-learning is a model-free, off-policy algorithm that directly learns the optimal action-value function. The update rule is:

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α[r + γ max_a' Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)]

Deep Q-Networks (DQN), introduced by Mnih et al. (2015), extend Q-learning to high-dimensional state spaces by approximating Q-values with neural networks. Key innovations include experience replay and target networks, which stabilize training.

Variants of DQN include Double DQN (which addresses overestimation bias), Dueling DQN (which separates state value and advantage), and Prioritized Experience Replay (which samples important transitions more frequently).

3.2 Policy Gradient Methods

Policy gradient methods directly parameterize and optimize the policy. The REINFORCE algorithm uses the policy gradient theorem:

∇J(θ) = E[Σ ∇log π_θ(a_t|s_t) G_t]

where G_t is the return from time t. While unbiased, REINFORCE suffers from high variance.

Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) constrains policy updates to a trust region, ensuring monotonic improvement. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) simplifies TRPO by using a clipped surrogate objective:

L^CLIP(θ) = E[min(r_t(θ)A_t, clip(r_t(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε)A_t)]

PPO has become the de facto standard for many RL applications due to its simplicity and effectiveness.

4. Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

Model-based RL learns a model of the environment's dynamics and uses it for planning. This approach can be significantly more sample-efficient than model-free methods.

4.1 Learned Dynamics Models

The agent learns a transition model T(s'|s,a) from collected experience. This model can be used for:
- Planning ahead using tree search or trajectory optimization
- Generating synthetic experience for model-free training (Dyna architecture)
- Computing analytical policy gradients through the learned model

Neural network dynamics models have shown impressive results, particularly when combined with ensemble methods for uncertainty estimation. The PETS algorithm (Chua et al., 2018) uses probabilistic ensembles for robust planning.

4.2 World Models

World Models (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018) learn a compressed representation of the environment using a variational autoencoder (VAE) and a recurrent network (MDN-RNN). The agent can then train entirely in the learned "dream" environment, dramatically reducing the need for real interactions.

MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020) learns a model that predicts only the quantities relevant for planning: the reward, the value function, and the policy. This approach achieved superhuman performance in Atari, chess, shogi, and Go without knowledge of the game rules.

4.3 Sample Efficiency

Model-based methods typically achieve 10-100x better sample efficiency than model-free methods. However, they can suffer from model bias—errors in the learned model that compound over long planning horizons. Techniques to mitigate this include short planning horizons, model uncertainty estimation, and model-predictive control (MPC).

5. Actor-Critic Architectures

Actor-critic methods combine the benefits of policy gradient (actor) and value function (critic) approaches. The critic estimates the value function, which is used to reduce variance in the policy gradient estimate.

5.1 Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C/A3C)

A3C (Mnih et al., 2016) uses multiple parallel actors to collect experience asynchronously. Each actor maintains a local copy of the policy and updates a shared global model. The advantage function A(s,a) = Q(s,a) - V(s) is used to reduce variance.

A2C is the synchronous version, which collects experience from all actors before performing a single update. Despite its simplicity, A2C often matches or exceeds A3C's performance.

5.2 Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)

SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018) is an off-policy actor-critic algorithm that maximizes both the expected reward and the policy entropy:

J(π) = E[Σ R(s_t, a_t) + α H(π(·|s_t))]

The entropy term encourages exploration and improves robustness. SAC is particularly effective for continuous control tasks and has become a standard baseline in robotics.

5.3 TD3

Twin Delayed DDPG (TD3) addresses overestimation in actor-critic methods through three key techniques: twin critics (taking the minimum of two Q-value estimates), delayed policy updates, and target policy smoothing. TD3 achieves state-of-the-art results on continuous control benchmarks.

6. Applications in Robotics

6.1 Locomotion

RL has achieved remarkable results in robot locomotion. Policies trained in simulation can enable quadruped robots to walk, run, and traverse challenging terrain. Recent work has demonstrated:
- Legged locomotion on uneven terrain using proprioceptive feedback
- Parkour-like behaviors including jumping and climbing
- Energy-efficient gaits that adapt to different surfaces

Key challenges include sim-to-real transfer (bridging the reality gap between simulated and real environments) and robustness to perturbations.

6.2 Manipulation

Robot manipulation remains one of the most challenging RL applications. Tasks range from simple pick-and-place to complex dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered hands. Important advances include:
- Learning grasping policies from raw visual input
- Multi-step assembly tasks using hierarchical RL
- Tool use and object manipulation with dexterous hands
- Contact-rich manipulation using tactile sensing

6.3 Navigation

Autonomous navigation combines perception, planning, and control. RL agents learn to navigate in complex environments using visual observations:
- Indoor navigation in previously unseen environments
- Outdoor navigation for autonomous vehicles
- Drone navigation in cluttered spaces
- Social navigation respecting human conventions

7. Open Challenges and Future Directions

7.1 Sample Efficiency

Despite significant progress, RL algorithms still require millions of interactions to learn complex tasks. Improving sample efficiency through better exploration, transfer learning, and meta-learning remains a critical research direction.

7.2 Sim-to-Real Transfer

Policies trained in simulation often fail when deployed on real robots due to the sim-to-real gap. Domain randomization, system identification, and adaptive methods aim to address this challenge, but robust transfer remains an open problem.

7.3 Safe Exploration

In robotics, unsafe exploration can damage the robot or its environment. Constrained RL, safe exploration algorithms, and formal safety guarantees are active areas of research.

7.4 Multi-Agent Systems

Many real-world scenarios involve multiple agents that must coordinate or compete. Multi-agent RL faces challenges in non-stationarity, credit assignment, and communication.

8. Conclusion

Reinforcement learning has made remarkable progress in robotics applications. From locomotion to manipulation to navigation, RL agents are increasingly capable of learning complex behaviors. However, significant challenges remain in sample efficiency, safety, and generalization. Future work should focus on bridging the sim-to-real gap, enabling lifelong learning, and developing algorithms that can reliably operate in unstructured real-world environments.
